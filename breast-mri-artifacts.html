INTRODUCTION

Breast MRI is among the most sensitive imaging modalities for detecting invasive cancer and high-risk precursor lesions. Its performance depends on consistent, high-quality acquisition of multiple pulse sequences—most critically, dynamic contrast-enhanced (DCE) imaging, diffusion-weighted imaging (DWI), and fat-suppressed T2-weighted sequences. Despite advances in hardware and reconstruction, artifacts remain a frequent source of interpretive difficulty, variability in lesion conspicuity, and false-positive or false-negative findings.

Recognizing MRI artifacts is fundamental to confident interpretation. Because artifacts alter image appearance in predictable, physics-based ways, understanding their causes allows radiologists and technologists to distinguish spurious findings from true pathology—avoiding both overcalls and missed lesions. This article reviews the most impactful artifacts encountered in breast MRI, provides a systematic approach to recognition and troubleshooting, and highlights emerging mitigation strategies.

OVERVIEW OF MAJOR ARTIFACTS

Table 1 summarizes the major artifact categories, their primary causes, characteristic appearances, and first-line solutions. Several important practical caveats apply to the solutions listed.

For B₀ inhomogeneity and fat saturation failures, not all scanners have active shimming capability. On systems without active shimming, the volume (box) shim remains the primary tool and should be carefully optimized — particularly the shim box placement and size — before attributing fat saturation failure to an irreducible field problem. Where dual-transmit RF systems are available, these should be leveraged first, as they address the underlying field non-uniformity rather than working around it.

For fat saturation failures, STIR and Dixon-based sequences are effective fallbacks but carry important trade-offs. STIR imposes a significant SNR penalty and its interaction with contrast timing requires care. Dixon techniques improve robustness to B₀ variation but also have SNR implications that should be understood before wholesale protocol substitution. The first line of defense remains shimming optimization, repositioning, and hardware utilization — substituting sequences should be a considered second step.

For susceptibility artifacts from metallic hardware, spin-echo and MARS sequences reduce artifact but at a significant cost: longer acquisition times, reduced spatial resolution, and generally poorer image quality. These are meaningful trade-offs in a time-sensitive, multi-sequence breast MRI protocol and should be treated as last-resort tools rather than routine solutions.

For aliasing, in addition to FOV expansion and oversampling, saturation bands placed outside the FOV boundary can suppress signal from out-of-field structures — such as the arms or contralateral breast — before they are sampled and can alias into the image. This is a practical and underutilized tool particularly useful when FOV expansion would compromise resolution.

B₀ INHOMOGENEITY

Key Points

  • Arises from susceptibility differences at breast-lung interfaces; worse at 3T
  • EPI-DWI most affected: geometric distortion, lesion mislocation, unreliable ADC
  • Can obscure posterior cancers and DCIS
  • Box/volume shim is the primary tool on systems without active shimming
  • Fix: shim optimization first; dielectric pads; dual-transmit if available; B₀-corrected reconstruction

The main magnetic field must remain uniform across the imaging volume for accurate spatial encoding. In breast MRI, complex tissue interfaces among parenchyma, fat, chest wall, and air-filled lungs create susceptibility gradients that disrupt this uniformity. Effects scale with field strength; artifacts at 3T typically exceed those at 1.5T. Metallic implants—biopsy markers, surgical clips, chemotherapy ports—create localized field distortions that compound the problem.

B₀ inhomogeneity manifests as low-frequency shading (one breast appearing darker), geometric distortion near the posterior breast and chest wall, and on DWI, lesion displacement of several millimeters with unreliable ADC values. On subtraction images, it can create pseudoenhancement or exaggerate kinetic curves. Clinically, these artifacts may obscure posterior lesions, including small invasive cancers or DCIS foci, particularly in high-risk screening populations.

The first corrective step is shim optimization. Many clinical systems offer only volume (box) shimming, which fits a shim volume to the region of interest and adjusts gradient offsets to minimize field variation within it. Careful placement and sizing of the shim box around the breast tissue — excluding as much air and lung as possible — can meaningfully improve homogeneity and should be performed routinely. On systems with active shimming (higher-order shim coils), automated shimming algorithms can compensate for more complex field patterns. Dual-transmit systems address the related problem of B₁ field inhomogeneity and can improve fat saturation uniformity. Dielectric pads placed adjacent to the breast alter the local dielectric environment and can partially counteract susceptibility-driven field variations, particularly at 3T.

SUSCEPTIBILITY ARTIFACTS (METALLIC HARDWARE)

Key Points

  • Caused by biopsy clips, surgical markers, ports, tissue expanders
  • Appear as signal voids with surrounding geometric distortion (blooming)
  • May obscure tumor bed assessment post-biopsy or post-lumpectomy
  • Can cause secondary effects including regional fat-saturation failure
  • SE and MARS sequences reduce artifact but are slow with significant image quality trade-offs

Susceptibility artifacts arise from local magnetic field distortions caused by metallic objects. Biopsy clips, surgical markers, chemotherapy ports, tissue expanders, and even dense calcifications create abrupt changes in the local magnetic field. These distortions cause signal dephasing, resulting in signal voids that extend well beyond the physical size of the object (blooming artifact). The effect is most pronounced on gradient-echo sequences and EPI-based DWI.

Beyond the focal signal void, metallic hardware can cause broader effects on image quality. Field distortion from a port or large clip may disrupt fat saturation throughout the adjacent breast, even in regions distant from the hardware itself. Clinically, susceptibility artifacts can obscure assessment of the tumor bed in post-biopsy or post-lumpectomy patients.

Spin-echo sequences reduce susceptibility artifact relative to gradient-echo by refocusing dephased spins, and switching from GRE to SE acquisitions is the first practical step when blooming is problematic. Metal-artifact-reduction sequences (MARS) and view-angle tilting (VAT) can further reduce geometric distortion but come at a significant cost: substantially longer acquisition times, reduced spatial resolution, and generally poorer overall image quality. In the context of a time-constrained, multi-sequence breast MRI protocol, these are meaningful trade-offs that should be weighed against the diagnostic question being asked rather than applied reflexively.

MOTION ARTIFACTS

Key Points

  • Respiratory motion causes ghosting along phase-encode direction
  • Characteristic zebra stripe pattern from periodic motion
  • Can obscure lesion margins even when lesions are real
  • Subtraction misregistration may mimic or mask enhancement
  • Fix: high temporal resolution DCE; compressed sensing; patient coaching; retrospective correction

Motion artifacts arise from respiratory, cardiac, and voluntary movement during acquisition. Unlike head MRI, breast imaging cannot easily leverage breath-hold techniques for long DCE acquisitions. Normal respiration causes cyclic anterior-posterior displacement; anxiety or discomfort amplifies motion. Long acquisition times and sequences with long echo trains (such as single-shot EPI DWI) are particularly vulnerable.

Motion manifests as blurring of lesion margins, ghosting along the phase-encode direction, and a characteristic zebra stripe pattern from periodic respiratory motion. Importantly, motion artifact can obscure the margins of real lesions, complicating assessment of morphology and extent even when the underlying pathology is genuine. On subtraction imaging, misregistration between pre- and post-contrast acquisitions can both mimic enhancement and mask true enhancing lesions.

High-temporal-resolution DCE sequences reduce motion sensitivity by shortening each acquisition window. Compressed sensing and radial k-space trajectories (such as GRASP and radial VIBE) are particularly motion-robust due to their incoherent undersampling patterns and continuous data acquisition. Clear patient instructions before scanning and comfortable positioning reduce voluntary motion. Retrospective motion correction tools are increasingly available as post-processing options on modern platforms.

ALIASING (WRAP-AROUND) ARTIFACTS

Key Points

  • Occurs when anatomy outside FOV wraps into the imaging volume
  • Skin line, arms, contralateral breast are common sources
  • Key recognition: aliased signal follows contour of source structure
  • Phase-encode direction determines wrap orientation
  • Fix: increase FOV; oversampling; saturation bands outside FOV; no-phase-wrap option

Aliasing occurs when anatomy outside the field of view is undersampled and wraps into the imaging volume, projecting over the breast tissue. In breast MRI, wrap-around typically introduces signal from the skin, arms, lateral chest wall, or even the contralateral breast depending on FOV and phase-encode direction. The artifact is most common when the FOV is too small in the phase-encode direction.

The key to recognizing aliasing is that the wrapped signal follows the contour of its source structure. High-intensity skin signal wrapping into the center of the image will trace the same curve as the skin line at the breast periphery. Understanding the phase-encode direction for a given acquisition helps predict where wrap-around will appear and from what source.

Solutions include increasing the FOV, enabling no-phase-wrap (oversampling) options, or placing saturation bands outside the FOV boundary. Saturation bands suppress signal from out-of-field structures — such as the arms or contralateral breast — before they are sampled, preventing them from aliasing into the image. This is a practical and often underutilized tool, particularly useful when FOV expansion would compromise spatial resolution or scan time.

CHEMICAL SHIFT ARTIFACTS

Chemical shift artifact arises from the difference in resonant frequency between fat and water protons (~3.5 ppm, approximately 220 Hz at 1.5T and 440 Hz at 3T). In the frequency-encode direction, fat and water signals are spatially offset by a number of pixels determined by the ratio of this frequency difference to the receiver bandwidth. At low bandwidths the offset is large; at high bandwidths it is reduced at the cost of SNR.

In breast MRI, chemical shift artifact is most conspicuous at fat-water interfaces — the anterior and posterior breast margins, and around fat-containing structures such as lymph nodes. On localizer sequences, which are typically acquired with limited bandwidth to maximize SNR, the artifact can be particularly prominent. The dark band on one side of an interface and bright band on the other are the hallmark appearance and should not be mistaken for edema or pathological enhancement.

FIGURE 12 Chemical Shift
Chemical shift artifact on localizer image. Sagittal localizer image demonstrates prominent dark and bright bands at fat-water interfaces at the anterior and posterior breast margins. Localizer sequences are typically acquired with limited receiver bandwidth, which increases the frequency-encode pixel shift between fat and water and renders chemical shift artifact more conspicuous than on diagnostic sequences. The spatial offset is proportional to field strength and inversely proportional to bandwidth.
GEOMETRIC DISTORTION

Geometric distortion results from spatial encoding errors caused by gradient nonlinearity and B₀ inhomogeneity. All MRI scanners have inherent gradient field nonlinearities that increase toward the periphery of the gradient coil. Manufacturers apply post-processing correction algorithms that remap peripheral voxels to compensate, but residual distortion and interpolation artifacts remain visible at the extremities of the imaging volume — visible as curvilinear bowing at image corners when the image is windowed to show its full extent.

In breast MRI, geometric distortion is particularly relevant for radiotherapy planning and image-guided biopsy targeting, where accurate spatial correspondence between MRI and treatment coordinates is essential. EPI-based sequences (primarily DWI) are most susceptible due to their low effective bandwidth in the phase-encode direction. Field map-based distortion correction improves spatial fidelity in these sequences.

PARTIAL VOLUME ARTIFACTS

Key Points

  • Occurs when multiple structures occupy the same voxel
  • Breast tissue folding on itself is a common cause in prone positioning
  • Apparent signal represents average of overlapping tissues
  • Can mimic lesions or obscure small findings
  • Fix: thinner slices; always scroll through adjacent slices to confirm

Partial volume artifact occurs when two or more tissue types occupy the same imaging voxel, producing a signal that represents their average rather than either tissue individually. In breast MRI, this commonly occurs when breast tissue folds upon itself during prone positioning, particularly in patients with larger or ptotic breasts. The folded tissue creates apparent signal abnormalities on a single slice that resolve when scrolling through the volume.

Recognition requires reviewing adjacent slices. A finding caused by partial volume averaging from tissue folding will change dramatically or disappear on neighboring slices, whereas a true lesion maintains consistent appearance across multiple slices. Thinner slice acquisitions reduce partial volume effects but increase scan time and may reduce signal-to-noise ratio. When partial volume artifact is suspected, careful review of the entire stack is essential before characterizing any finding.

FIGURE 16 Partial Volume / Breast Fold

Sagittal image demonstrates abrupt signal change at a breast fold where tissue curves back on itself, creating a plane of partial volume averaging that can appear as an artifactual interface or pseudolesion. This is distinct from true incomplete coverage: the breast is within the FOV, but the geometry of the fold results in a single voxel containing signal from tissues with differing contrast. The artifactual nature is confirmed by scrolling through the stack, where the apparent lesion resolves as a fold rather than a discrete structure.
IMPLANT-RELATED ARTIFACTS

Silicone and saline implants introduce multiple artifact sources. The large volume of non-tissue material creates field inhomogeneity at implant margins, disrupting fat saturation and causing geometric distortion in adjacent breast tissue. Implant shells and intracapsular folds generate susceptibility effects. Standard DCE breast MRI protocols are insufficient for implant integrity assessment; dedicated implant protocols employing IDEAL or silicone-specific inversion recovery sequences are required.

PREVENTION AND QUALITY CONTROL

Quality Control Checklist

Patient Preparation

  • Educate on importance of remaining still; minimize motion
  • Ensure comfortable prone positioning; consider sedation for anxious patients

Positioning

  • Verify symmetric breast placement within coil
  • Minimize air gaps between breast and coil
  • Check for tissue folding, especially in larger breasts

Protocol Verification

  • Confirm complete coverage on scouts (axilla, lateral, inferior)
  • Optimize shim box placement before contrast administration
  • Verify fat saturation on initial sequences before contrast
  • Check for ghosting/motion on early acquisitions
  • Place saturation bands outside FOV if aliasing is anticipated

Equipment

  • Regular coil testing and B₀ shim calibration
  • RF shielding integrity checks
EMERGING SOLUTIONS

Artificial intelligence is transforming artifact mitigation. Deep learning–based reconstruction technologies integrate denoising, parallel imaging calibration, and motion correction into unified pipelines, substantially enhancing image quality while reducing acquisition times. AI-based artifact detection systems can flag sequences with motion, fat-saturation failure, or shim errors in real time, enabling technologists to repeat acquisitions before the patient leaves the scanner.

Prospective motion correction using optical tracking or navigator-based approaches shows promise for reducing respiratory and voluntary motion. At higher field strengths, including emerging 7T breast MRI, advanced shimming methods and multi-transmit RF systems will be essential. Dixon-based fat-water separation has become increasingly robust and is gradually replacing frequency-selective fat saturation in many protocols. Compressed sensing and 3D stack-of-stars trajectories enable ultrafast acquisitions that minimize motion sensitivity.

CONCLUSION

Artifacts in breast MRI are pervasive and multifactorial, arising from field inhomogeneity, incomplete fat suppression, metallic hardware, motion, aliasing, partial volume effects, and various technical factors. These artifacts reduce diagnostic accuracy, complicate staging, and may delay or misdirect treatment. However, because artifacts alter image appearance in predictable ways, understanding their physics-based origins enables confident differentiation of spurious findings from true pathology.

Modern MRI systems, advanced reconstruction algorithms, and protocol standardization enable substantial artifact mitigation when properly applied. Radiologists and technologists must work collaboratively to recognize artifacts promptly, troubleshoot root causes, and adopt standardized acquisition and quality-assurance workflows. Future developments—including AI-driven artifact detection, real-time correction, and patient-personalized acquisition strategies—promise further improvements in diagnostic confidence for early cancer detection and treatment planning.
